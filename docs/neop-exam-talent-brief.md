# NEOP Comprehension Exam — briefing for Talent Acquisition

**AMR KC, interfacility operation**
For review by Talent Acquisition, Requisition and HR before first use.

**This document is not legal advice.** It sets out what the instrument is, how
it is used and the design decisions behind it, so that HR and employment
counsel can review it. Sections marked **DECISION** need somebody with
authority to answer them before an applicant sits this.

---

## In one paragraph

It is called a comprehension exam because that is what it measures.
Applicants to the KC interfacility operation read a written description of the
job and then answer 41 questions about it — patient care at newly-qualified EMT
level, comprehension of what they just read, and a set of unscored questions
about what they want from a career. **It does not decide who is hired.** The
interview does that, as it always has. What the exam produces is information
for two people: the interviewer, who gets specific answers to open a
conversation with, and the educator, who knows before a new hire's first shift
which parts of orientation actually need to land for them.

---

## What problem it solves

Two, and neither is "screen people out."

**Applicants do not know what this job is.** Most have never been told what an
interfacility operation does all day, so "ambulance" means what they have seen
on a scene. They find out what the work actually is after they are hired, and
some of them leave. The reading fixes the information gap before anybody
invests a day in anybody: some applicants will read it and withdraw, and that
is the point working, not a cost.

**The educator starts blind.** Today a new hire arrives at academy and their
instructor learns what they need to work on by watching them struggle through
it. This gives that instructor a head start — before day one, in writing.

---

## How it is used, precisely

| Stage | What the exam does |
|---|---|
| Application | Candidate reads the job description and sits the exam. Untimed reading, 35-minute exam. |
| Interview | Interviewer sees the answers and a suggested follow-up question for each. **The interview makes the selection decision.** |
| Academy | Educator sees which topics scored low and plans orientation accordingly. |

**It is not a hurdle.** There is no score at which the software declines
anybody, no ranking that decides an offer, and nothing on the results screen is
coloured red. Marks below 70% (patient care) or 75% (our operation) are shown
as topics worth covering, not as failures.

---

## What the candidate is told

Before they start, in writing, on the same page as the Start button:

- What the job actually is — including that it is not 911 scene response, that
  most transports run at ordinary road speed, and that this position is not a
  waiting list for a future 911 opening.
- **That the exam does not decide whether they are hired**, and that what they
  write helps us plan their training if they join us.
- That the preference section is not scored and no answer there costs them the
  job.
- How to ask for an adjustment — extra time, a screen reader, anything else —
  before they start.

They then tick and sign an integrity statement, which is stored with their
attempt along with a fingerprint of its exact wording, so a later edit to that
statement cannot change what a past applicant is recorded as having agreed to.

---

## The three sections

**Patient care — 12 questions, scored.** Held deliberately at the level of a
newly-qualified EMT and no higher. EMT and paramedic applicants sit the same
section, so paramedic-only material would mark an EMT down for the certificate
they hold rather than the person they are. Nothing in it is specific to our
operation — an item about managing lines and drains looks clinical but actually
tests a job the applicant has not been given yet.

**Our operation — 16 questions, scored.** Every item comes from a section of
the job description they read minutes earlier, and the software refuses to
build the question bank if any item references material the description does
not contain. A question whose answer is not in the reading would test whether
somebody already works here.

**What you want — 13 questions, NOT scored.** No answer key exists for these —
not "we choose not to score them", but no correct answer is stored in the
database and the grading function skips them. Every option is a reasonable
thing for a good candidate to say. They exist so the interview starts from
something concrete rather than "so, tell us why you want to work here", and no
answer in this section is a reason to decline anybody.

## What HR and counsel will want to know

### DECISION 1 — is this pre-offer or post-offer?

This is the question that matters most, and it is not ours to answer.

An exam sat by applicants is treated as a **selection procedure** once a score
influences a hiring decision — regardless of what anybody intended it to be. If
scores are visible to the people making the hire, "we only meant it as a
teaching aid" is not the test that gets applied.

Two clean postures:

1. **Diagnostic only.** Administer at or after a conditional offer, or keep
   results out of the hiring decision-makers' hands until the selection
   decision is made. Cleanest match to the stated purpose.
2. **Part of selection, and said so.** Keep it pre-offer and visible, and treat
   it as a selection procedure properly — which means validation evidence and
   adverse-impact monitoring, the ordinary obligations for any employment test.

What does not work is using it as (2) while describing it as (1).

### DECISION 2 — who may see results?

In the software, results are administrator-only in both the app and the
database; field training officers cannot see them. Whether interviewers should,
and at what point, follows from Decision 1.

### DECISION 3 — sign-off on candidate-facing wording

Two sentences shown to applicants are promises the organisation has to keep:
that the exam does not decide whether they are hired, and that an adjustment
can be arranged on request. Both should be read by whoever owns that promise.

### What is already built in

- **Job-relatedness.** The operations section is comprehension of our own
  written job description. The clinical section is at the level of the
  certificate the applicant already holds.
- **Consistency.** Every candidate gets the same shape of exam — same number of
  questions from each section — and every candidate is asked all 13 preference
  questions, so no two are compared on questions only one was asked.
- **Prohibited topics.** One list, shared with the AEMT programme, covering
  what may never be asked at any stage: family and childcare arrangements,
  pregnancy, age, health or disability, religion or national origin, marital
  status, arrest record, and second jobs. Nothing in this exam asks about any
  of them.
- **Accommodation route**, offered before the timed section begins.
- **Records.** Attempts are retained, not deleted. Resetting somebody's attempt
  marks it void and records who did it and when — the integrity statement, the
  signature and the timestamp all survive. Nothing is erased at the moment
  somebody exercises discretion over it.
- **Reviewable content.** Every question can be read by an administrator with
  the answer key, exported for subject-matter review, and comes with its own
  statistics once enough people have sat it — how many answered it correctly,
  and whether it distinguishes stronger candidates from weaker ones at all.

### What is not built in

- **No demographic data is collected.** The exam records a name, an email and
  answers. That means adverse-impact monitoring cannot be done from this data
  alone; if it is required, it has to come from HR's own records.
- **No validation study has been done.** The content argument above is a
  content-validity argument, and a reasonable one, but it is not a formal study.
  Whether one is needed follows from Decision 1.

## Questions you may be asked, and straight answers

**"Does this replace the interview?"**
No. It informs it. The interview makes the decision.

**"Can somebody fail?"**
No. There is no score that declines anybody. Low marks route to orientation
planning.

**"What if a candidate says they want to go to 911 eventually?"**
That is a legitimate thing to want, and the exam treats it as a conversation
rather than a mark against them. Sometimes the right outcome is pointing
somebody at the Linn County posting instead — better for them and for us than
finding out in month four.

**"Could a candidate work out the answers we want?"**
On the scored half, yes — by reading the job description, which is exactly what
we want them to do. The preference section is deliberately built so that no
option is obviously the wanted one: every choice is defensible, and several ask
what somebody has already done rather than what they intend.

**"How long does it take?"**
Reading is untimed. The exam is 35 minutes, one attempt.

**"What if their attempt goes wrong — a dead phone, a dropped connection?"**
An administrator can reset it in one click. The original attempt is kept on
record rather than deleted.
